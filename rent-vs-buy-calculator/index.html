How to use the rent vs buy calculator

Enter the price of a home you would realistically buy and the rent for a comparable place. Add your down payment, mortgage rate, property tax, insurance, maintenance and any HOA dues. Then choose how fast you expect home prices and rents to grow, what you could earn by investing instead, and how many years you plan to stay. The result compares where your money ends up under each choice.

How it works

The calculator runs month by month. A buyer pays the mortgage, property tax, insurance, maintenance and HOA dues. A renter pays rent and renter's insurance. Whoever has the lower housing cost in a given month invests the difference at your investment return, so both sides spend the same total each month. On day one, the renter also invests the cash the buyer would have spent on the down payment and closing costs.

At the end of each year the calculator compares net worth. The buyer's net worth is the home's value after selling costs, minus the remaining loan, plus any invested savings. The renter's net worth is the investment balance. The break-even year is the first year buying comes out ahead.

Worked example

With the default numbers, a $400,000 home with 20% down at 6.5% costs about $2,020 a month in principal and interest, plus taxes, insurance and upkeep. Rent of $2,200 is cheaper at first, so the renter invests the savings along with the $92,000 that would have gone into the down payment and closing costs. After ten years, renting and investing is slightly ahead, at about $242,000 against $234,000 for buying, because selling costs and early interest weigh on the buyer. Stay longer, or assume faster price growth, and buying pulls ahead. Change the investment return or the number of years to see how sensitive the answer is.

Things the calculator leaves out

Tax deductions for mortgage interest and property tax are not included, nor are capital gains taxes on investments or a home sale. Moving costs, furnishing and the value of stability or flexibility are personal and hard to price. Treat the result as a starting point and test pessimistic numbers too, such as slower price growth or a higher rate. If you are buying to rent out, use the rental property calculator or the mortgage calculator instead.

Frequently asked questions

How does the rent vs buy calculator decide which is better?

It compares your net worth under each choice at the end of the period you pick. The buyer owns a home, minus the remaining loan and selling costs. The renter invests the down payment and closing costs, plus any monthly savings from renting, at the investment return you enter.

What is the break-even year?

It is the first year in which buying leaves you with more net worth than renting. If you expect to move before then, renting is likely cheaper. If it never appears, renting comes out ahead over the whole period with your numbers.

Why does the investment return matter so much?

Money not tied up in a house can be invested. A higher expected return makes renting and investing more attractive, while faster home price growth favors buying. Try a few realistic values for both.

Does it include tax benefits of owning?

No. Mortgage interest and property tax deductions depend on your tax situation and whether you itemize, so they are left out. If you benefit from them, buying will look somewhat better than shown.

Are maintenance costs included?

Yes. Enter annual maintenance as a percentage of the home value. One to two percent a year is a common rule of thumb for budgeting repairs and upkeep.
